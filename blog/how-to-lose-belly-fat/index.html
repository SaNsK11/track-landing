How to Lose Belly Fat: What Actually Works

You want a flatter stomach and a leaner physique. You're already putting in work at the gym. So why does the belly fat stick around like it owns the place? Here's the truth — and more importantly, here's what to do about it.

Why Belly Fat Is So Stubborn (And What That Really Means)

Before you can beat something, you need to understand it. There are two types of fat sitting around your midsection. Subcutaneous fat lives just under the skin — it's the stuff you can pinch. Visceral fat sits deeper, wrapping around your organs. Both respond to the same fundamental approach, but visceral fat is actually more metabolically active, which means it tends to shift faster when you get your habits right.

Here's the hard truth: you cannot spot-reduce fat. No amount of crunches will melt fat off your stomach specifically. Fat loss happens systemically — your body decides where it comes from based on genetics, hormones, and overall energy balance. What you can control is creating the conditions for your body to lose fat overall, and your belly will follow.

The good news? The strategy that strips belly fat is the same one that builds a physique you're proud of. There's no special protocol. There's just doing the right things consistently.

If your diet isn't dialled in, no training programme on earth will give you the results you want. This isn't a lecture — it's just physics.

Create a Calorie Deficit (But Not a Stupid One)

Fat loss requires a calorie deficit. That means consuming fewer calories than your body burns. Aim for a moderate deficit of 300–500 calories per day. This is the sweet spot — aggressive enough to produce real fat loss (roughly 0.5–1 lb per week), conservative enough to preserve the muscle you've worked for.

Slashing calories to 1,000 a day might sound like a shortcut, but it backfires. You'll lose muscle, tank your energy, and your metabolism will downshift to compensate. Play the long game.

Prioritise Protein

Protein is non-negotiable for fat loss. It keeps you full, preserves lean muscle mass during a deficit, and has a higher thermic effect than carbs or fat — meaning your body burns more calories just digesting it. Target 0.7–1g of protein per pound of bodyweight daily. For a 180 lb person, that's 126–180g of protein per day.

Practical sources: chicken breast, eggs, Greek yoghurt, cottage cheese, lean beef, tinned tuna, and protein shakes when you're in a pinch. Build every meal around a protein source first, then add your carbs and fats around it.

Stop Obsessing Over Carbs

Carbs are not the enemy. Sugar-heavy, processed foods eaten in excess are the problem. Complex carbs — oats, rice, sweet potatoes, fruit — support your training and keep you fuelled. Manage your overall calorie intake, hit your protein targets, and don't demonise an entire macronutrient.

Strength Training: The Most Underrated Fat-Loss Tool

Most people trying to lose belly fat default to cardio. That's backwards. Strength training should be the cornerstone of your fat-loss plan — here's why.

Muscle tissue is metabolically expensive. The more of it you carry, the more calories your body burns at rest. Building and maintaining muscle through resistance training raises your basal metabolic rate, making fat loss easier to sustain over time. Cardio burns calories during the session. Strength training keeps burning them long after you've left the gym.

The Programme That Works

You don't need a complicated plan. Three to four days of full-body or upper/lower strength training per week is enough to build muscle, preserve it during a deficit, and dramatically change how your body looks.

Focus on compound movements — exercises that recruit multiple muscle groups simultaneously. These burn more calories, trigger more muscle growth, and give you the best return on your time:

  • Squats — 3–4 sets of 6–10 reps
  • Deadlifts — 3 sets of 5–8 reps
  • Bench Press or Push-Ups — 3–4 sets of 8–12 reps
  • Barbell or Dumbbell Rows — 3–4 sets of 8–12 reps
  • Overhead Press — 3 sets of 8–10 reps

Progressive overload is the mechanism that makes this work. Each week, aim to add a small amount of weight or squeeze out an extra rep. If you're lifting the same weight for the same reps month after month, you're maintaining, not progressing. Log your sessions so you can track this — more on that at the end.

Cardio: Use It Smartly, Not Desperately

Cardio has a role in a fat-loss plan — but it's a supporting actor, not the lead. Used correctly, it accelerates the calorie deficit without chewing into recovery capacity.

LISS: Your Low-Effort, High-Return Option

Low-Intensity Steady-State cardio — think brisk walking, cycling, or light jogging — is easy to recover from and adds up fast. Aim for 8,000–10,000 steps per day as a baseline. This alone can account for 300–500 extra calories burned daily without spiking hunger or hammering your joints.

If you want to add dedicated cardio sessions, two to three 30-minute LISS sessions per week is a solid starting point. Keep them separate from your strength sessions if possible, or do them afterwards.

HIIT: Efficient But Not Magic

High-Intensity Interval Training burns a lot of calories in a short window and can be useful when time is tight. A 20-minute HIIT session — 30 seconds of hard effort, 30 seconds of rest, repeated for 16–20 rounds — can be highly effective. But it's taxing on the body. Limit HIIT to one or two sessions per week maximum, and make sure your recovery is solid. More is not better here.

Sleep and Stress: The Hidden Variables Wrecking Your Progress

You can eat perfectly and train hard and still stall on fat loss if your sleep and stress are out of control. This isn't soft advice — it's physiology.

Sleep More to Lose More

Sleep deprivation directly increases ghrelin (the hunger hormone) and decreases leptin (the satiety hormone). One bad night won't derail you, but chronic poor sleep will make you hungrier, reduce your willpower, impair your muscle recovery, and slow your fat loss. Aim for 7–9 hours per night. Treat sleep like a training variable, because it is one.

Manage Cortisol

Chronic stress elevates cortisol — a hormone that, over time, promotes fat storage, particularly around the abdomen. You can't eliminate stress, but you can manage it. Regular training helps. So does limiting alcohol (which also disrupts sleep and adds empty calories), protecting downtime, and not training like an elite athlete when you're running on fumes.

If your life is chaotic right now, the most useful thing you can do for your belly fat might be improving your sleep before adding another gym session.

Consistency Over Perfection: The Real Secret

Here's where most people go wrong. They follow a plan hard for two weeks, hit a rough patch, blow up their diet one weekend, and decide the whole thing isn't working. Then they start over. Then repeat.

Fat loss — especially losing stubborn belly fat — takes time. Real, meaningful change in your body composition typically takes three to six months of consistent effort. That's not a long time in the grand scheme of your life, but it requires patience that most people don't have because they've been sold the idea of quick fixes.

The goal is to be consistent 80–90% of the time. That allows for life to happen — a birthday dinner, a holiday, a week when work is brutal — without it derailing your progress. One bad day doesn't undo a week of good work. What kills progress is letting one bad day become a bad week.

Track your food for at least four to six weeks. Not because you'll do it forever, but because most people are genuinely shocked by how many calories they're eating without realising it. Awareness alone changes behaviour.

And track your training. Progressive overload only works if you know what you lifted last time. Keep a log, review it weekly, and make small adjustments as you go.

Putting It All Together

Losing belly fat isn't mysterious. It's a calorie deficit maintained over time, supported by high-protein nutrition, consistent strength training, smart cardio, adequate sleep, and managed stress. None of these elements are optional — they work together as a system.

Start here: 1. Calculate your maintenance calories and subtract 300–400 2. Hit 0.8g of protein per pound of bodyweight daily 3. Strength train three to four days per week using compound lifts 4. Walk 8,000–10,000 steps per day 5. Prioritise seven to eight hours of sleep 6. Log everything and review weekly

That's the plan. It's not complicated, but it does require commitment.
