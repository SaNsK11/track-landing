Building Muscle Over 30: What Changes and What to Do

You want to build muscle. You're not 22 anymore, and you're starting to wonder if that matters. It does — but not in the way you think. Here's exactly what shifts after 30 and how to use that knowledge to keep making real progress.

What Actually Changes After 30

Let's be straight with you: your body at 30, 35, or 40 is not broken. But it is different, and training like nothing has changed is one of the fastest ways to spin your wheels or get hurt.

Testosterone and Anabolic Hormones

After 30, testosterone levels decline gradually — roughly 1% per year on average. That's not a cliff, it's a slope. The practical effect is that recovery takes slightly longer and building muscle may require more deliberate effort than it did in your early twenties. The good news? Resistance training is one of the most reliable ways to support healthy testosterone levels. You're not fighting biology — you're working with it.

Muscle Protein Synthesis

One of the more significant physiological changes is a reduction in muscle protein synthesis (MPS) sensitivity. Younger muscles respond robustly to even modest protein intakes and training stimuli. After 30, your muscles become slightly less efficient at converting protein into new muscle tissue, particularly at lower protein doses. The fix is straightforward: eat more protein per meal and train with enough volume and intensity to force an adaptation. More on both shortly.

Recovery and Connective Tissue

Joints, tendons, and ligaments don't recover as fast as they did a decade ago. This isn't a reason to train less hard — it's a reason to train more intelligently. Sleep, nutrition, and programming matter more now than they ever did. Take this seriously and you'll be lifting productively for decades. Ignore it and you'll spend months nursing avoidable injuries.

The Training Approach That Works

This is where most articles go vague. Not this one.

Train 3–4 Days Per Week

For most people over 30 with jobs, families, and actual lives, three to four full days of resistance training per week is the sweet spot. It delivers enough stimulus for meaningful muscle growth while giving your body adequate time to recover. Five or six days works for some people, but more sessions means each session needs to be lower in volume — and that's a trickier balance to manage.

A simple structure that works: - 3 days: Full body Monday / Wednesday / Friday - 4 days: Upper / Lower split — Upper Mon & Thu, Lower Tue & Fri

Prioritise Compound Lifts

Squats, deadlifts, bench press, overhead press, barbell or dumbbell rows, and pull-ups. These movements recruit the most muscle mass, produce the strongest hormonal response, and give you the best return on your training time. Build your sessions around 2–3 compound lifts, then add 2–3 accessory exercises to target weak points or add volume to specific muscle groups.

Sets, Reps, and Progressive Overload

For building muscle — also called hypertrophy training — the evidence-backed rep range is 6–20 reps per set, with most of your work sitting between 8 and 15. Aim for 10–20 hard sets per muscle group per week, spread across your sessions.

A practical starting point: - Compound lifts: 3–4 sets of 6–10 reps - Accessory work: 3 sets of 10–15 reps - Rest periods: 2–3 minutes between heavy sets, 60–90 seconds between accessory sets

The non-negotiable: progressive overload. Add weight, reps, or sets over time. If you're lifting the same weights you were three months ago, you're maintaining — not building. Track every session so you always know what you did last week and what you need to beat.

Don't Skip Deload Weeks

Every 4–8 weeks, take a planned deload. Reduce your training volume by roughly 40–50% for one week — same exercises, lighter weights or fewer sets. This isn't weakness, it's strategy. Connective tissue, the nervous system, and your motivation all benefit. Most people come back the following week lifting more than they did before the deload.

Nutrition: The Part Most People Get Wrong

You can have a flawless training programme and make almost no progress if your nutrition isn't supporting it. After 30, two things matter most.

Eat Enough Protein — More Than You Think

Given the reduced muscle protein synthesis sensitivity mentioned earlier, hitting your protein targets becomes more important, not less. Aim for 0.7–1 gram of protein per pound of bodyweight per day (roughly 1.6–2.2g per kg). For a 180 lb (82 kg) person, that's 125–180g of protein daily.

Spread that across 3–4 meals. Research suggests that consuming at least 40g of protein per meal may be more effective for muscle protein synthesis in people over 30 compared to smaller doses — another shift from the conventional "30g per meal" advice aimed at younger lifters.

Solid protein sources: chicken, beef, eggs, Greek yoghurt, cottage cheese, salmon, and protein shakes when whole food isn't practical.

Eat in a Slight Calorie Surplus

To build muscle, you need to eat slightly more than you burn — typically 200–400 calories above your maintenance level. This creates the energy environment your body needs to synthesise new muscle tissue without excessive fat gain. If you're gaining more than 1–2 lbs per month, dial the surplus back. If you're seeing no scale movement over 3–4 weeks and training hard, eat a little more.

If you're carrying extra body fat, you can still build muscle while eating at maintenance or a small deficit — especially if you're newer to training. Just know that progress will be slower than it would be in a surplus.

Recovery: The Underrated Muscle Builder

Sleep is where muscle is actually built. During deep sleep, your body releases growth hormone and carries out the repair and growth processes that your training initiated. Adults who consistently sleep less than 7 hours show measurably lower anabolic hormone levels and slower recovery times. If you're doing everything right in the gym and kitchen but sleeping 5–6 hours, you're leaving significant gains on the table.

Aim for 7–9 hours per night. Treat this as part of your training programme, not a nice-to-have.

Stress management matters too. Chronically elevated cortisol — the stress hormone — competes directly with testosterone and blunts the muscle-building response to training. You don't need to meditate on a mountain, but regular exercise (which you're already doing), adequate sleep, and not running on empty every day will keep cortisol in a range that supports your goals.

The Mindset Shift That Changes Everything

Building muscle after 30 requires you to play a longer game — and that's actually where you win. The people who build impressive physiques in their thirties and forties aren't doing secret programmes or taking exotic supplements. They're doing the basics, consistently, over years.

Set a 12-month goal, not a 6-week one. Focus on getting 1% better each session. Stop comparing your progress to what 19-year-olds post on social media. Track your lifts so you can see real progress over real time — because progress in the gym is slow enough that it's easy to miss without a record of where you started.

You're not too old. You're not past your prime. You're just getting started — and you have the discipline to actually stick with it this time.
